\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

This chapter establishes the research context and problem statement of SENTINEL---a Cognitive Two-Tier Architecture automating threat detection and incident response within Security Operations Centers (SOCs)---and states the objectives, research questions, scope, and contributions of the thesis.

\section{Background and Motivation}

Cyber attacks have grown in both volume and sophistication, and the defensive burden they impose has grown with them. A mid-sized Security Operations Centre now ingests millions of log events every day~\cite{siem2021}, the overwhelming majority of which record ordinary activity. Because the cost of missing an intrusion is far greater than the cost of examining a harmless event, monitoring systems are deliberately tuned toward sensitivity, and false-alarm rates consequently exceed 80\%~\cite{alertfatigue2022}. The asymmetry compounds: every additional sensor raises the alert count, while the number of specialists able to read those alerts stays fixed. The binding constraint of a modern SOC is therefore not the ability to detect. It is the allocation of scarce human attention, which cannot be multiplied by buying more servers.

Against that pressure, defenders currently rely on three layers of countermeasure. At the perimeter, firewalls and intrusion detection systems match traffic against static signatures and deterministic rules, blocking what has been described in advance. Behind them, SIEM platforms centralise telemetry from across the estate and correlate it with rules written by the security team. Above both, SOAR platforms execute pre-programmed playbooks so that a recognised situation triggers a scripted response without waiting for a person~\cite{soar2025}. Everything these layers cannot settle arrives on an analyst's queue, and there the work becomes manual: the analyst retrieves the raw log, reconstructs what the source address did before and after the flagged moment, checks the destination against the internal asset inventory, looks up the technique the behaviour resembles, and only then decides whether to block, dismiss, or escalate. Each of those steps is reading and cross-referencing, and reading does not parallelise.

Two weaknesses follow directly, and they are the reasons this thesis exists. The first is \textbf{time}. An analyst needs minutes to work through a single alert while the stream arrives continuously, so any queue whose arrival rate exceeds its service rate grows without bound---and the backlog is cleared not by finishing it but by abandoning it. The second is \textbf{context}. The evidence that decides a case is frequently spread across many separate records and buried inside long, unstructured text fields, so the decision requires holding a lengthy and heterogeneous context in mind at once. Static rules cannot express that at all, because what is needed is not a pattern to be matched but a judgement to be formed. This is precisely why the existing automation does not lift the burden: signatures and playbooks recognise only what has already been described, they fail against variants outside their descriptions, and while they can classify an event, they cannot explain why it matters. Large Language Models offer exactly the missing capability---reading an event in its context and explaining it in language a person can act on---but placing one directly in an operational data stream introduces three new costs. Each act of reasoning takes seconds while data keeps arriving~\cite{vaswani2017}. The reasoning layer itself becomes a target, because a log field can carry wording an attacker composed deliberately to steer the model~\cite{promptinjection2023}, and whatever the model concludes must be provably unaltered if it is to serve as evidence. And internal logs typically may not leave the organisation, so the model has to run on site, on finite hardware~\cite{nist80207}.

The founding observation of this research is the cost gap between two kinds of decision. A decision made by a statistical rule takes less than a millisecond; a decision made by a language model takes seconds. That gap of four to five orders of magnitude makes routing every event through the most expensive tier a systematic waste, when the vast majority of events can be settled by very cheap checks. The objective of this thesis is therefore to design, build, and empirically evaluate \textbf{SENTINEL}, a two-tier architecture built on the principle of \textit{placing cheap decisions before expensive ones}: a fast filtering tier that settles most of the traffic outright, and a reasoning tier called upon only for what remains, operating behind protective guardrails and a cryptographically sealed audit trail, entirely on local infrastructure. What makes this a research problem rather than an engineering one is that each of the three costs above must be \textit{measured}, on real data, with metrics that cannot flatter themselves.

\section{Objectives, Research Questions, Scope and Contributions}
\label{sec:rq}

The general objective is operationalised as three research tasks. Each is paired one-to-one with a research question and with the contribution it is intended to produce, and Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments_evaluation} reports results under the same three headings, so that objective, question, and evidence remain aligned throughout.

\textbf{First --- filtration economics.} The objective is to construct a stateless dual-stage filtration pipeline operating at wire-speed, so that the majority of telemetry is resolved at the cheap tier without consuming GPU resources. The corresponding question, \textbf{RQ1}, asks: \textit{How can a multi-tiered architecture be designed to autonomously offload security telemetry at wire-speed, thereby addressing the latency bottlenecks and resource consumption inherent in Large Language Models?} The intended contribution is to propose and quantify the principle of placing cheap decisions before expensive ones, and to demonstrate by measurement that the offload rate is \textbf{a function of the attack base rate of the stream} rather than a constant of the system---a property not stated explicitly in the related literature.

\textbf{Second --- security of the reasoning layer.} The objective is to protect the language model structurally rather than probabilistically, and to seal the forensic record so that any alteration is detectable. The corresponding question, \textbf{RQ2}, asks: \textit{What methodologies and security mechanisms can effectively withstand adversarial risks (such as log-substrate prompt injection) to protect the AI's reasoning flow, while simultaneously ensuring the integrity and tamper-evidence of forensic audit trails?} The intended contribution is a defence based on Delimited Data Encapsulation with random cryptographic nonces, whose essential property is that it \textbf{does not depend on the block rate of any classifier}: encapsulated content is processed as passive text regardless of what it says, unlike probabilistic semantic filters whose effectiveness degrades against novel variants~\cite{owasp_llm2025}.

\textbf{Third --- reasoning, attribution, and human workload.} The objective is a stateful reasoning engine that retrieves domain knowledge, cites the evidence it used, and routes to a human the cases that warrant one. The corresponding question, \textbf{RQ3}, asks: \textit{How can stateful reasoning capabilities and domain-specific knowledge retrieval be integrated into an AI Agent to automate intrusion analysis, attribute attack techniques, and generate transparent forensic reports, thereby reducing and accurately triaging the workload requiring human analysts?} The intended contribution is to replace rigid SOAR playbooks with a stateful agent over a STIX-aligned MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge base, coupled with a deterministic RAG-anchoring guardrail requiring every emitted technique identifier to be present in that batch's retrieved documents---turning hallucination control from a hope placed in the model into a verifiable architectural constraint.

\textbf{Scope.} The object of study is the SENTINEL architecture itself: a Tier 1 stateless filter, a Tier 1.75 semantic cache, a Tier 2 stateful agent engine running a locally hosted quantised model, a hybrid retrieval system over MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge, and the cryptographic controls protecting both. Empirical evaluation rests on \textbf{two primary datasets}---CSE-CIC-IDS2018~\cite{ids2018} for wide-area network telemetry and CSIC 2010~\cite{csic2010} for real-world HTTP Layer 7 attacks. Two supplementary sources, DAPT2020~\cite{dapt2020} for multi-stage intrusion chains and a synthetic zero-day set, are included as \textbf{proof-of-concept evidence only} and support no contribution claim. Adversarial evaluation uses 823 samples partitioned by provenance and never merged: \textbf{703 from published corpora} (AdvBench~\cite{advbench2023}, deepset~\cite{deepset2023}, jackhhao~\cite{jackhhao2023}) as the headline denominator, 80 author-composed samples, and 40 cross-check samples. The system is deployed on a single local workstation operating fully air-gapped, and automated responses are restricted to blocking IP addresses, logging, or escalating to an analyst; physical infrastructure intervention is out of scope.
